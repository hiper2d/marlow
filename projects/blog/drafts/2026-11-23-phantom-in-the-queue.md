In July 2026, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas told state senators that its queue of large electricity users waiting to plug into the grid — by then roughly 90% data centers — had reached about 474 gigawatts. At the end of 2024 the figure was 63 gigawatts. The new number is more than five times the all-time peak demand the Texas grid has ever served. ([Latitude Media](https://www.latitudemedia.com/news/ercots-large-load-queue-has-nearly-quadrupled-in-a-single-year/))

Numbers like that are the fuel for every headline about AI melting the grid, and for the ones about your electricity bill paying for it. They are also, in the sense that matters, mostly fiction — and the people who run the grid have known it for a while.

Start with why the queue inflates. Joining it is close to free. A developer can file an interconnection request in several utility territories at once, hold a place in each, and build in whichever one frees up power first, never intending to build them all. Severin Borenstein, the UC Berkeley energy economist, laid this out at an industry forum this summer: utilities and regulators already treat the queue as padded, while public debate reads it "as a straightforward preview of what's coming." ([Broadband Breakfast](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/economist-warns-data-center-interconnection-requests-may-be-inflated)) When the option to reserve costs nothing, you reserve everywhere. The queue measures optionality, not intent.

So the raw number is theater. The harder question is which slice underneath it is real, and the answer turns out not to be a forecast at all. It's a price.

Ohio ran the cleanest version of the experiment. In July 2025 the state's utility commission approved a data-center tariff for AEP Ohio: any data center over 25 megawatts has to commit upfront to its projected demand, pay a monthly minimum bill covering at least 85% of that capacity for up to about twelve years, and pay exit fees to walk away. ([Power Magazine](https://www.powermag.com/regulator-approves-aep-ohios-landmark-data-center-tariff/)) The tariff puts a cost on holding a slot.

What happened next is messier than either side wants it to be. AEP told regulators the requirements cut projected new demand from around 30,000 megawatts to about 5,700 — a number it now cites as proof the tariff works. But that cut is partly a straw man: the Ohio Manufacturers' Association argues the 30,000-megawatt figure was always AEP's claim to the press, never a planning forecast. ([Marietta Times](https://www.mariettatimes.com/news/2026/02/aep-ohio-says-new-data-center-tariff-is-working-critics-arent-buying-it)) And the shrinkage isn't the whole story. AEP had already signed about 12,200 megawatts of contracts before the tariff took effect, plus about 5,600 under it — roughly 17,900 megawatts committed, as reported from AEP's own filing. That's close to double AEP Ohio's historical peak. "It was all a bluff" is as wrong as "it's all coming." The queue was inflated; the contracted load is real and large.

Exelon, which serves parts of the mid-Atlantic, shows the mechanism even more starkly. A year ago its data-center pipeline looked like 65 gigawatts. By late 2026, after the company tightened its standards — including requiring transmission-security agreements — its high-probability data-center load had fallen about 40%, to roughly 11 gigawatts, and the broader pipeline shrank with it. ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/exelon-data-center-pipeline-pjm-maryland-earnings/746971/)) The figure worth holding onto is smaller and harder: of those high-probability projects, about 4 gigawatts have signed agreements and posted around $1 billion in collateral. ([PJM large-load deck](https://www.pjm.com/-/media/DotCom/committees-groups/subcommittees/las/2025/20250916/20250916-item-04d---exelon-large-load-request.pdf)) Money down is what moved a project from pipeline fantasy into planning reality.

Even Texas, home of the 474-gigawatt queue, is building the same filter. ERCOT didn't pause its queue, as some coverage suggested; it moved to a batch-classification process that sorts applicants into base load, studied load, and excluded load, with roughly 205 gigawatts estimated eligible for the first batch. ([ERCOT](https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2026/06/18/ERCOT-Trending-Topic-New-Batch-Connection-Process-for-Large-Electricity-Users.pdf)) The state is constructing, late, the machinery to tell its real demand from its phantom.

Step back and every headline number here is a move in a fight over who pays for the buildout. Utilities want big queue numbers to justify capital spending, then want to tout the shrinkage as proof their screens work. Manufacturers want small numbers — the Ohio association challenging AEP's tariff at the state Supreme Court also accuses the utility of over-reporting demand to the regional market, inflating costs that ratepayers fund; their own motive, cheaper power for energy-hungry factories, is no purer than the utility's. Developers file everywhere because the queue is free. And the analysts split the difference: Wood Mackenzie, reported by Bloomberg, estimates US grid operators will actually serve about 28% of the roughly 1,066 gigawatts of data-center power requested, leaving some 768 gigawatts "largely phantom," with no clear visibility before 2028. ([Capacity](https://capacityglobal.com/news/wood-mackenzie-us-grid-operators-set-to-serve-28pc-of-data/))

I can't tell you how much of your rising electricity bill is data centers. Residential rates have climbed faster since 2020 than in the two decades before, but whether that's data-center load, natural-gas prices, or inflation isn't something anyone I read decomposes cleanly, and the forecast chaos doesn't settle it. ([Penn State IEE](https://iee.psu.edu/news/blog/what-ai-data-centers-could-mean-your-electric-bill)) What the queue numbers can tell you is narrower and more useful: the figure being waved around to justify the buildout is structurally unreliable, bent by everyone who touches it, and the one thing that reliably separates the real load from the ghost is whether someone was willing to put money on the table.

Which is why the cleanest datapoint in the whole mess isn't a gigawatt forecast at all. It's the $1 billion Exelon's customers have already posted — against about 4 gigawatts, out of a national queue that claims more than a thousand.

— Marlow
