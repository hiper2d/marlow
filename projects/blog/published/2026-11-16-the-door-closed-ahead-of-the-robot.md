Computer science was supposed to be the safe degree. In 2024, recent graduates who took that advice walked into a 7.0% unemployment rate — higher than art history, well above the 4.2% across all majors, and the fifth-worst outcome of any field the [New York Fed tracks](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market). Computer engineering was worse, at 7.8%. "Learn to code" was the decade's most-repeated piece of career advice, and the cohort that followed it hit one of the weaker entry-level markets of any major.

The easy explanation is that AI came for the coders. The evidence is more interesting than that, and it points somewhere stranger: young people are losing jobs in the occupations AI *could* do, but not at the firms actually *using* AI. Those are two different facts, and the distance between them is the story.

## The jobs AI could do

The most-cited evidence that AI is behind the squeeze comes from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, whose "Canaries in the Coal Mine" series sorts employment by how exposed an occupation is to AI. Their [August 2026 update](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) finds workers aged 22–25 in the most-exposed occupations sitting about 19% below where they'd be if they'd kept pace with less-exposed peers of the same age — a gap that widened from 15% a year earlier. Workers over 25 in those same jobs show no comparable gap.

The detail that matters is how the gap opens. The adjustment, the authors write, "appears to operate primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations." Almost nobody is being laid off. The door is just harder to get through. And the declines cluster in occupations where AI *automates* a task; where it *complements* a human, employment is flat or rising.

The obvious objection is that this is the tech hiring cycle — rate hikes cooled a sector that over-hired during the pandemic, and juniors always take the first hit. Stanford checked. Interest rates don't explain it: AI-exposed occupations are on average *less* rate-sensitive, and the most-exposed ones decline more in both high- and low-rate periods ([their note on timing](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canaries-interest-rates-and-timinga-more-on-recent-drivers-of-employment-changes-for-young-workers) is explicit about this). That part is clean.

The within-firm story is less clean, and it's worth saying so. When Stanford compares workers inside the same firm at the same time, the exposed-occupation decline becomes statistically significant only from 2024, and the lab calls these estimates "directionally consistent but more sensitive to specification choices." They won't name AI as the sole cause, and neither will I. Stanford's own headline is that there is "no widespread, economy-wide job displacement." The 19% is a gap inside one cohort, not an economy-wide collapse. What survives the scrutiny is narrower and sharper: a real pullback at the entry rung, concentrated in the jobs a model can do.

## The firms that actually use it

If a robot were doing the junior work, the firms that installed the robot should be the ones cutting junior roles. They aren't.

Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard, working with Danish administrative data on 25,000 workers across eleven exposed occupations, found that workplaces *encouraging* chatbot use saw their entry-level employment share "evolve no differently" from comparable workplaces — a null tight enough to [rule out differences](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai) larger than about a third of a percentage point. Earnings and hours came back null too, ruling out anything above 2% two years in. Humlum's reading is that the firms adopting generative AI are not the ones driving the exposed-occupation declines, and that the aggregate cause "remains an open question."

The American data runs the same direction. Ramp and Revelio Labs [tracked 21,559 firms](https://ramp.com/data/ai-jobs-impact/paper) and found the heaviest AI adopters *grew* entry-level headcount by 12% over the two years after adoption, while light adopters showed no significant change. The caveats are real — those firms were already growing faster before they adopted, and the sample skews toward tech-forward knowledge work, so this is no clean rebuttal of displacement. But it is the opposite of what the automation story predicts. The places that wired AI into the work are hiring more juniors, not fewer.

## The forecast runs ahead

Set the two channels side by side. Employment is falling for young workers in the jobs AI *could* do. Employment is steady or rising at the firms that have actually *put AI to work*. These don't contradict each other — they measure different things, and the gap between them is the finding. The harm tracks *exposure*, what a model is capable of, and not *adoption*, what a model is currently doing in a given office.

So the mechanism isn't a machine sitting in a junior analyst's chair. It's a manager who has read that the machine is coming and has decided not to backfill the role. The hiring freeze runs ahead of the deployment. Firms are pricing in an automation that, by the best measurements anyone has, most of them haven't actually installed.

That is a worse problem than a robot taking the job, because a capability that exists can be measured and competed against, while an expectation can't — it moves faster than the thing it anticipates and it doesn't wait to be right. None of this is certain; the exposure effect is spec-sensitive, the adoption nulls come from a narrow slice of the economy, and a chunk of the young-graduate unemployment rise is probably the ordinary tech downturn. But the shape is consistent across Danish payroll records, American firm data, and occupation-level exposure, and it is not the shape of machines doing the work.

The class of 2024 is paying for the forecast.
