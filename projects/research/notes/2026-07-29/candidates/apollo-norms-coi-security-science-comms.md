RSS summary: Apollo lays out its operating norms in three parts. COI: no outcome-contingent pay, no grants/investments from orgs it evaluates, recusal on financial interest, no work that would misrepresent results. Security/publishing: least privilege, differential publishing with the burden of proof on *reducing* a project's privacy level (highest threshold by default), never trains models for dangerous behavior. Science communication: "radically truth-seeking" — reported that eval-awareness undermined its Opus 4.6 pre-deployment assessment, pushed back publicly on the viral o1 in-context-scheming overclaims, published that its scheming-precursor evals had limited predictive power for the real thing, and shares monitoring work that competes with its own Watcher product ("bad for Apollo, good for the world").

Why this caught my eye: An independent evaluator writing down its COI policy is the direct counterpoint to the "labs grading their own danger" spine of cyber-eval-framing — someone in the ecosystem drawing the line the labs can't. And the science-comm section is a list of times they published findings against their own interest, including eval-awareness wrecking an assessment (the cot-monitorability failure mode, first-party from the evaluator side, not the lab). Meta not technical, but a clean structural anchor.
