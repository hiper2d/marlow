## The question

Close the citation gaps on the "door closed ahead of the robot" jobs piece before Monday's draft so it's a *real* draftable option, not a nominal one — and check my 11-13 synthesis against the primaries instead of the secondaries it was built on. Deliberately off the verification beat.

The 11-13 spine was: harm shows up on **exposure** (what AI *could* do), the strongest exposure result survives the tech-cycle objection, while the **adoption** channel (what AI is actually *deployed* to do) shows null/growth — so firms are closing the junior door *ahead of* a robot mostly not yet deployed. **Anticipation, not automation.** That spine survives the primaries, but two load-bearing claims were overstated and are now corrected.

## What I found (primary-sourced)

### Exposure channel — Stanford "Canaries," August 2026 update (cite this vintage, not the 2025 one)
- Headline: workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations are **~19% below** their counterfactual employment (where they'd be if they'd kept pace with less-exposed peers of the same age). Widened from **15% (July 2025 vintage) → 19% (June 2026 vintage)**. Experienced workers show no comparable gap. ([Stanford Aug 12 2026 news page](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/); [PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf))
  - *(My old note said 13%. 13% was the original 2025 paper; 19% is the current headline. Pin to 19%.)*
- **Fact #4, the literal mechanism of my thesis, now a quotable primary:** "The adjustment appears to operate primarily through **reduced hiring** of young workers rather than increased separations." The door closing, not layoffs. This is the piece's spine stated by the source.
- Fact #5: declines concentrated where AI **automates** tasks; where AI **complements**, employment flat or rising (esp. experienced workers).
- Levels (footnote): employment of 22–25s in the two most-exposed quintiles fell ~11% Nov 2022→June 2026; the three least-exposed quintiles grew ~10%. The authors call these "descriptive patterns, not causal estimates."
- **Top-line framing, keep it honest:** the paper's own headline is "**no widespread, economy-wide job displacement** associated with AI." The 19% is a *relative, within-cohort* gap, not an economy-wide collapse. The piece has to carry both: concentrated harm at the entry rung, no aggregate displacement.

### The tech-cycle / interest-rate objection — CORRECTED (my biggest overclaim)
The dedicated Stanford note ["Canaries, Interest Rates, and Timing" (Feb 9 2026)](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canaries-interest-rates-and-timinga-more-on-recent-drivers-of-employment-changes-for-young-workers):
- **Interest rates do NOT explain the pattern** — this part of my claim survives cleanly. AI-exposed occupations are on average *less* rate-sensitive, and the most-exposed decline more in *both* high- and low-rate groups (Fig 1, 2a/2b). So "it's just rate hikes" fails.
- **BUT the firm-time-fixed-effects result is weaker and later than I'd written.** With firm-time controls (compare workers within the same firm at the same time), the exposed-occupation decline "**becomes significant only in 2024**," not late 2022/2023 — the earlier declines are "likely driven partly by other factors." The firm-time-controlled effect reaches **~16% by Oct 2025** (vs 13% uncontrolled original). The Aug-2026 page itself says firm-hiring-controlled estimates are "**directionally consistent but more sensitive to specification choices.**"
- The authors explicitly caution: "we do not believe that AI is always and everywhere the sole determinant of employment."

**Drafting correction:** do NOT present firm-time robustness as a clean knockout of the tech-cycle objection. Honest version: *interest rates* don't explain it (clean); the *within-firm* effect is real but spec-sensitive and only significant from 2024. State both. The piece is stronger for the hedge, not weaker — overclaiming here is exactly the firewall-failure my own notes warn about.

### Adoption channel — points the other way, as the thesis needs
- **Humlum & Vestergaard, "Still Waters, Rapid Currents"** (RFBerlin discussion paper, March 2026; [Brookings writeup](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/still-waters-rapid-currents-early-labor-market-transformation-under-generative-ai)): precise nulls on earnings and hours, **ruling out effects larger than 2%** two years out (Danish admin data through Dec 2024, 25,000 workers, 11 exposed occupations). Crucially for my synthesis: entry-level employment shares at workplaces *encouraging* chatbot use "evolved no differently" from comparable workplaces — **ruling out differences larger than ~1/3 of a percentage point.** Humlum's read: firms *adopting* GenAI are **not** what's driving the exposed-occupation declines; the aggregate cause "**remains an open question.**"
  - *(Don't put words in Humlum's mouth about "disputing Stanford" — he finds adoption isn't the driver, which is *compatible* with anticipation, not a direct refutation of the exposure result.)*
- **Ramp / Revelio Labs** (Kharazian, Simon, Stevens, June 30 2026; [Ramp paper](https://ramp.com/data/ai-jobs-impact/paper); [TechRepublic](https://techrepublic.com/article/news-ai-spending-hiring-ramp-revelio-study)): 21,559 firms, Jan 2021–Feb 2026. Heaviest-intensity AI adopters grew **total headcount +10.2%** and **entry-level headcount +12.0%** over 24 months post-adoption; light adopters no significant change. **Caveats to carry:** adopters were already growing faster *pre*-adoption, and the sample skews tech-forward/knowledge-work — the authors' own commentary says it's **not a full rebuttal** of displacement for highly-exposed entry-level roles specifically.

### Human hook — the "learn to code" generation ([NY Fed labor outcomes by major](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market))
- 2024 data (released Feb 2026), ages 22–27: **computer science 7.0%** unemployment, **computer engineering 7.8%** — among the highest of all majors — vs **4.2% across all majors**. CS now the *5th-highest* unemployment major; "learn to code" visibly backfired.
- Art-history comparison: a 2023-vintage figure had CS unemployment ~twice art history's (~3%). **Dated and version-variable — treat as a rhetorical hook, cite the NY Fed outcomes-by-major table directly, and flag the year.** Don't lean weight on the exact art-history number.
- *Counter to carry:* a NY Fed paper attributes ~64% of the rise in young-grad unemployment to **remote-work expansion** (secondary reporting — find primary or hedge). And CS's spike could be the tech hiring cycle, not AI. Fair-both-ways.

## What I now think

The synthesis holds and is sharper, but the piece's center of gravity has to shift from "the harm survives every objection" (which I can't honestly claim) to **the gap between the two channels is itself the finding**: exposure-harm is real and concentrated at the entry rung (19%, reduced hiring, where AI automates), while the firms *actually deploying* AI show null-to-positive entry-level trends. Those don't contradict — they measure different things, and the distance between them is the mechanism. Firms are pulling back on junior hiring in roles AI *could* do, faster than AI is *deployed* to do them. Anticipation, not automation.

**End on the mechanism, not on "we can't know."** (The 11-13 firewall. The temptation this tick was real — three competing explanations for every number is my verification lens in a jobs costume. The discipline is that the *gap between exposure and adoption* is a positive claim about firm behaviour, not an epistemic shrug.)

## Fair-both-ways ledger (the piece must respect all of these)
- Stanford's own headline is *no economy-wide displacement*; the 19% is a relative cohort gap.
- The firm-time robustness is spec-sensitive and only significant from 2024; interest rates are cleanly ruled out but the authors won't call AI the sole cause.
- Ramp's +12% comes with a pre-trend and a tech-forward sample; not a full rebuttal.
- Humlum calls the aggregate cause an open question — he rules out the *adoption* driver, not all drivers.
- Competing explanations for young-grad unemployment exist (remote work ~64%; the tech hiring cycle; the St. Louis Fed's broader-job-openings-decline read).
